{\LARGE\bfseries Do energy price shocks feed into euro area wages?}\par
\vspace{0.8em}

\section*{Summary thoughts}
\begin{itemize}
\item Consider an energy shock that is structural/permanent: a 10 pp quarter-on-quarter increase in the price of oil or gas; the outcome is the percentage point change in the annual growth rate of wages (both negotiated and actual from National Accounts)
\item No effects of supply shocks on relevant variables; country heterogeneity resembles what we know of national collective bargaining systems
\item No signs of second round effects: we reach this conclusion by looking the transmission of energy supply shocks to inflation. Consistent with Corsello and Foschi on impact, absorbed (more for oil than gas) in the first two years, and --importantly-- no effects up to three years
\item Previous literature indicates that the passthrough is high in high-inflation environment (Corsello and Foschi) or tight labour market conditions (Beqiraj, Bik and Di Bartolomeo, 2026). While we do not find direct evidence for this, OLS inform us about overall energy price transmission including high-demand periods. There is a transmission to negotiated wages --especially in ...-- but yet no transmission to actual wages nor to HICP down the line
\item Techinicalities: baseline specification is a smoothed (actually, undersmoothed) IV-LPs \'{a} la Barnichon and Brownlees (2019) at quarterly frequency 
\item Techinicalities: negotiated wages are excluding one-off, but for FR, ES and BE; baseline specification is a smoothed (actually, undersmoothed) IV-LPs \'{a} la Barnichon and Brownlees (2019) at quarterly frequency 
\item Robust to: pre-COVID, unsmoothing, monthly instruments, reduced-form (oil IV is weak in some specifications)
\end{itemize}

%\section*{Why it matters}
%Whether energy-driven inflation spills over into wages is central to how persistent it turns out to be. For consumer prices, Corsello and Foschi (2026) find that oil supply shocks have short-lived effects, whereas gas supply shocks have stronger and more persistent ones, and that indirect effects are muted when inflation is low but become pronounced in high-inflation regimes. This note asks the same question for wages, distinguishing negotiated wages, set in collective agreements, from actual wages per hour worked.

%\section*{How we estimate it}
%We estimate local projections (Jordà, 2005) on quarterly data for the euro area and for Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands and Belgium, up to 12 quarters after the shock. The quarter-on-quarter change in the spot price of oil (in US dollars) or of natural gas (TTF) is instrumented with external supply shocks, as in Corsello and Foschi (2026): the oil supply news shocks of Mori and Peersman for oil and the gas supply shocks of Alessandri and Gazzani (2025) for gas (Stock and Watson, 2018). Responses are scaled to a 10\% increase in the price. The outcomes areyear-on-year growth in negotiated wages (ECB indicator) and in gross wages per hour worked (Eurostat national accounts); HICP inflation, hours worked and the unemployment rate are reported in the working document.

%The shocks are monthly, the wage data quarterly: the instrument is the quarterly average of the monthly shocks. To limit the erratic movements from one horizon to the next typical of local projections in short samples, the responses are smoothed across horizons as in Barnichon and Brownlees (2019), with confidence bands computed by undersmoothing. Following Corsello and Foschi (2026), both stages control for two lags of the outcome, of the price change, of log industrial production, of the unemployment rate and of the 1-year Bund yield, and include a dummy for 2020--2022 and its lags (Newey--West standard errors). The strength of the instruments is checked with the effective $F$ statistic of Montiel Olea and Pflueger (2013): \tbc{first-stage strength for oil and gas, euro area and countries}. As robustness checks, the estimates are repeated without smoothing, with the three monthly shocks of each quarter as separate instruments (Foroni, Marcellino and Schumacher, 2015) and on pre-2020 data only; reduced-form and OLS estimates are in the working document.
