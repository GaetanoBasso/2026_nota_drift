\section*{Why it matters}

Whether energy-driven inflation spills over into wages is central to how persistent it
turns out to be. For consumer prices, Corsello and Foschi (2026) find that oil supply
shocks have short-lived effects, whereas gas supply shocks have stronger and more
persistent ones, and that indirect effects are muted when inflation is low but become
pronounced in high-inflation regimes. This note asks the same question for wages,
distinguishing negotiated wages, set in collective agreements, from actual wages per
hour worked.

\section*{How we estimate it}

We estimate local projections (Jordà, 2005) on quarterly data for the euro area and for
Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the Netherlands and Belgium, up to 12 quarters after the
shock. The shock is the year-on-year change in the spot price of oil (in US dollars) or
of natural gas (TTF); responses are scaled to a 10 percentage point increase in that
change. The outcomes are year-on-year growth in negotiated wages (ECB indicator) and in
gross wages per hour worked (Eurostat national accounts); HICP inflation, hours worked
and the unemployment rate are reported in the working document.

Following Corsello and Foschi (2026), all regressions control for four lags of the
outcome and of the shock, of log industrial production, of the unemployment rate and of
the 1-year Bund yield, and include a dummy for 2020--2022. Estimates are country by
country (time series, Newey--West standard errors); pooled estimates for the six
countries (country fixed effects, employment weights, Driscoll--Kraay standard errors;
Driscoll and Kraay, 1998) are in the working document. As a robustness check, all
estimates are repeated on pre-2020 data only. Euro-area sample for negotiated wages
(shock dates): \smp{lp_og_EA_contr}.

Unlike Corsello and Foschi (2026), who identify oil and gas supply shocks with external
instruments, we use observed price changes, net of their own past dynamics and of the
controls: the responses are a reduced-form measure of pass-through, not the causal
effect of an identified supply shock.
